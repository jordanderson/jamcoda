How JamCoda works

Jamcorder produces one MIDI file per day with all your practice sessions. That’s fantastic, but if you play a lot and want to know exactly which songs you played, or follow your progress on one piece over time, you have to go through each recording and label it by hand. The magic of Jamcorder is that every note is captured as MIDI data, and that’s enough to recognize songs you’ve played before.

JamCoda compares short stretches of MIDI with examples from your annotations, finds the phrases it recognizes clearly, and joins them into the stretches you spent on each song. Where it had to fill in a passage without recognizing the song clearly, it marks the spot, so you know where to listen before accepting it. Each prediction you confirm or correct becomes an annotation, and the next model rebuild learns from it.

Six seconds at a time

JamCoda reads note pitches, start and release times, velocity, and sustain-pedal MIDI events. The song names come from the musician.

The model examines six seconds of playing at a time, moving forward one second for each new window. That gives it some musical context without blurring every change of song. Six seconds worked best in experiments with windows from four to eight seconds. Shorter windows notice sooner that the playing has moved off a song, but they lose slow, sparse pieces, which give a short window too few notes to recognize.

Each window gets a label for its center. A window covering 0–6 seconds describes what is playing at second 3; the next describes second 4. Those centers determine the initial song boundaries. A later step adjusts them to nearby notes.

What it remembers about the music

Each window becomes 53 numbers. Most describe which pitch classes are sounding: how much C, C♯, D, and so on. They are counted separately below middle C and from middle C upward, so bass and treble patterns don’t get mixed together. This approximates the separation between accompaniment and melody.

Longer notes and louder notes receive more weight. Other numbers describe register, density, dynamics, note lengths, silence, and the regularity of note starts. The last two groups describe the shape of the chords, from the intervals between notes sounding together, and the rhythmic texture, from how the gaps between note starts are spread. Two passages can use the same pitch classes and still differ in both. The model retains these summaries, rather than the exact sequence of notes.

The sustain pedal matters. When the musician releases a key with the pedal down, the model lets the note continue for up to 0.7 seconds, stopping sooner if the pedal lifts. Otherwise, lifting the hands to change position can look like silence even though the piano is still ringing.

An explicit tempo estimate was tried and removed. Slow practice and a faster performance of the same piece could look too different. Rhythm still enters through note density, duration and regularity, and through the spread of gaps between note starts, which describes rhythmic texture without settling on a tempo.

Feature calculations

Each register’s pitch-class profile is normalized separately to sum to one. A note’s contribution is its duration inside the window, weighted by sqrt(velocity/127). The pedal tail contributes half that weight; its length is capped at 0.6 seconds above C5 and 0.7 seconds elsewhere.

Silence is the fraction of quarter-second bins with no sounding notes. Linking stops at a ratio of 0.7, but a four-second gap inside a six-second window reaches only about 0.67. Short pauses can slip through.

Chord intervals split the window into seven bins, note which pitch classes sound together in each, and count every pair by interval class, one to six semitones; the six counts are normalized to sum to one. The gaps between consecutive note starts fall into ten bins, from under 80 milliseconds to four seconds and over, also normalized to sum to one.

Learning from the player’s annotations

Training keeps a sample of labeled windows, called prototypes. There is no neural network here: a prediction is based on resemblance to those stored examples. A separate __none__ label represents playing outside the named songs. These could be improvisations, ending flourishes, etc.

An annotation marks a stretch of working on one song, not a single take. Repeated takes, repeated sections and one hand at a time all belong to it, as long as the playing stays part of the song, and a pause of a few seconds doesn’t end it. It stops when the playing turns into noodling, and it ends on the song’s last notes, leaving out any flourish. Noodling after one song sounds much like noodling after another, and so does a closing flourish, which is why they are left unannotated: they are what teaches the model what “no song” sounds like.

The model learns what “no song” sounds like only from recordings the player has marked complete. In a recording that isn’t finished, an unlabeled stretch is often a song the player hasn’t labeled yet, and learning it as “no song” would teach the model to overlook that song. Marking a recording complete tells the model its unlabeled time really is something else, so it’s worth doing, but only once everything in it is labeled.

The model described here was trained from 1,430 annotations of 84 songs across 172 recordings. Those produced about 257,000 training windows, reduced to 15,421 prototypes. A song with lots of annotated playing gets more examples, allowing the model to remember different passages and different ways of playing them.

That also creates a bias. With more examples, a song has more chances to produce a close match, even when it is the wrong answer. Attempts to compensate for this have reduced accuracy. Giving sparsely annotated songs more examples remains the practical way to improve their coverage.

How many is enough? In a September 2026 test on this library, one labeled take of a song let the model find about half of that song’s later takes, three found about 78%, and five about 85%. Past ten, more takes of the same song rarely helped. Whole takes from different sessions teach it more than repeated passes from one sitting.

Prototype selection and scoring

The default prototype budget is 16,000, divided between labels in proportion to the square root of their training-window counts. The __none__ label has a separate cap of 60; giving it up to ten times as many barely changed its predictions, and began to cost real song time. Within each label, training takes an evenly spaced, deterministic sample. Caps and available examples mean the saved total can be below the budget.

Features are standardized using the training mean and standard deviation. Each label’s score is the negative Euclidean distance to its nearest prototype: a closer match gives a higher score.

The decoder compares the best score with the runner-up using (top − runner-up) / (|top| + |runner-up|). This margin measures how decisively one label won. It is not a probability that the song is correct.

Joining the recognizable parts

A distinctive phrase is easy to recognize. A few bars of repeated chords may fit several songs. Labeling each window independently would break a performance into fragments whenever the music became less distinctive.

JamCoda first looks for anchors: at least three consecutive windows where the same song wins by a margin of 0.15 or more. It then extends those runs into the less certain passages around them, then cleans up the result with these rules:

  1. Join anchors of the same song. If two neighboring anchor runs identify the same piece, extend into the gap from both ends. Silence and strong evidence for another label can still stop the extension.
  2. Limit extensions toward another song. Give an anchor two seconds of extension, then require its song to remain the top-scoring choice. This limit applies when a different-song anchor exists in that direction; open ends of the recording keep the ordinary linking checks.
  3. Let both sides advance. Competing extensions move one window at a time, so the earlier song doesn’t get first claim on the entire gap.
  4. Reconsider the gaps left over. A neighboring song can claim a gap if it ranks well enough on average across that stretch. This rescue pass skips gaps with the same song on both sides. The first rule already chose not to join that song across the gap, and the rescue should not undo that.
  5. Drop a short run inside another song. If a run of one song lasting 30 seconds or less has a single other song directly on both sides, leave it unlabeled. It is almost always the model misreading a moment of the surrounding piece, most often where a take restarts, so it is removed rather than left standing as the wrong song.

The fourth rule helps with passages the model partly recognizes. A song may rarely win a window but still rank near the top throughout its own performance. The rescue threshold is a mean rank of at most five: on average across the gap, no more than five labels score above the song.

The first four rules, called bridge linking, became the default in v2.11; the fifth arrived in v2.13. Each applies only to models rebuilt since it arrived, so updating the app never silently changes the predictions of a model you already have.

Joining two anchors of the same song is deliberate. Most gaps between them are pauses inside one stretch of work on the song: a restart, a page turn, a moment to think. Some are noodling between two separate stretches, and nothing tried so far tells the two apart from the playing alone. Half of those gaps are under five seconds, shorter than the window, so every window across one still contains some of the song on either side. The model therefore joins them, and marks the places where it did so on weak evidence, so the reviewer can decide.

Turning the result into suggestions

Consecutive windows with the same song label become a segment. By default, segments shorter than eight seconds or below 0.3 confidence are dropped, and remaining same-song segments separated by at most five seconds are merged. Boundaries then snap to nearby note starts and releases. A separate check can trim a closing arpeggio played after the final chord.

Before suggestions enter the review queue, the app removes time the player has already annotated. It also splits segments at device bookmarks and at recorded pauses of at least 30 seconds. These markers are applied after decoding; they don’t help the model identify the song.

The musician can confirm a suggestion, correct its name or boundaries, or reject it. Promoting an accepted suggestion makes it an annotation that the next rebuild can learn from. Rebuilding is a manual action. Marking a file complete clears its predictions and blocks further prediction runs, though Prediction Lab can still preview results on it, with or without that recording in the model’s training.

Each suggestion keeps a record of how the model reached it: which passages it recognized outright, which it filled in, and, for the filled-in ones, how far down the model’s list of guesses the song was. A filled-in passage of three seconds or more, where the song was on average no better than the model’s third guess, is marked worth a listen and hatched on the piano roll and on the whole-recording view. Reviewing a suggestion lists those passages, plays each with a few seconds either side, and offers to accept the suggestion with the passage cut out. That is the usual fix when a passage turns out to be noodling between two stretches of work on the song. On this library a recording gets about five marks, and about one in three covers a real error, so a mark is a place to listen, not a verdict.

The confidence shown on a suggestion is not the model’s raw margin, which can read backwards: a long, correct take averages toward an ordinary margin, while a short wrong burst can score high. Instead the app combines the margin with the segment’s length to estimate how likely you are to confirm it, using weights fitted to review decisions in this practice library. The 0.3 cutoff above still uses the raw margin.

How well it works

The figures below come from a fixed September 23, 2026 snapshot of this library. Each recording was predicted using a model trained on the other recordings. This leave-one-file-out evaluation checks recognition of a performance the model has not already stored.

Accuracy is reported on the 142 recordings marked complete. In an unfinished file, an unlabeled passage might be a song the player hasn’t gotten to yet. Scoring a correct prediction there as a false positive would measure annotation progress as much as model quality.

The scores follow what an annotation means: a stretch of work on one song. Two annotations of the same song less than three seconds apart count as one stretch, the gap a pause; a longer gap between them is noodling, and a prediction that carries the song across it has called that noodling the song.

The model rarely names the wrong song. Its mistakes are mostly about where a stretch ends: it treats noodling between two stretches of a song as the song, and runs the edges of a stretch a few seconds long. The first of these is what the review marks point to.

These scores don’t treat each annotation as a single take paired with at most one prediction. Scored that way, a prediction that joins two stretches of the same song loses a take even when all of its song time is right, and the model is penalized most for the pauses a player takes inside a stretch of practice.

Comparisons between versions use the same snapshot, and resample the recordings thousands of times to estimate how much of a change could be chance. These results describe this practice library; they don’t establish accuracy for other players or repertoires.

Where it still needs help

Noodling between two stretches of a song is counted as the song. To the model, a pause inside a stretch and noodling between two stretches look alike: unclear playing, often quiet, with the song on either side. Of the 246 such gaps of three seconds or more, 175 are carried across, and 89 of the 246 are shorter than the six-second window. A larger “no song” budget, stricter decoding settings, shorter windows and extra features for the middle of each window were all tried; each separated some noodling only by splitting as many real stretches of work. So the model joins them, and the review marks the passages it filled in on weak evidence.

Slow, sparse playing is hard to place. A slow hymn or a passage of sustained chords gives a six-second window few notes to go on. In the example recording the model stops O Come Emmanuel two and a half minutes early. In one recording from November 16, 2025, 24 of the 54 minutes predicted as Take Five are marked worth a listen: slow, sustained chords at about a third of the note density of the player’s own Take Five, where the model’s best guesses scatter across other slow pieces.

A weak passage can cut a stretch short or split it. When recognition fades partway through a performance, the model may stop the song too soon, or break one stretch into two predictions. In a recording from December 20, 2025, The Christmas Song comes out as two pieces with a 20-second gap between them. An experimental rescue setting helps some takes that stop too soon, but made no clear overall difference and stays off by default.

Drills can be mistaken for songs. Because “no song” is learned only from recordings marked complete, the model has fewer examples of scales, drills and noodling to learn from. In one recording, nine minutes of a two-note drill were labeled Beethoven’s 5th and Bridge Over Troubled Water.

Warm-up is a judgment call. In one recording, the annotations for The Entertainer begin 73 seconds in, while the model starts near the beginning. The disputed passage is warm-up on that piece. Whether warm-up is already work on the song or still noodling is the player’s call, and a consistent answer matters more to the model than which answer it is.

What improved

Increasing the number of stored examples improved coverage. Standardizing features made distances less sensitive to outliers. Accounting for pedal sustain reduced false silence, and snapping boundaries to notes removed some of the loose edges left by the one-second grid.

Bridge linking mostly improved endings. On the same snapshot, it raised F1 from 91.16% to 93.33% and reduced median ending error from 5.85 seconds late to 0.81 seconds late, with recall nearly unchanged.

v2.12 changed where the model learns “no song”. It used to take every unlabeled stretch of any recording with at least one annotation, including recordings the player hadn’t finished labeling. Those stretches are mostly real playing, often the very songs the model is trying to learn, so a new song’s unlabeled takes were being taught as “no song”. v2.12 learns “no song” only from recordings marked complete, and stores twice as many examples. For a song with one to three labeled takes and more still unlabeled, it finds 5 to 6 more of every 100 later takes. On this snapshot its overall score is unchanged (93.37% against 93.33%); on the larger September 21 library it rises from 91.8% to 92.9%.

v2.13 drops a short run of one song sitting inside another. In a Maple Leaf Rag session from June 17, 2026, the model had reported seven 9- to 15-second fragments of Bridge Over Troubled Water. Raising the eight-second minimum to catch them would also discard real short takes, so the rule looks for the shape instead. Across the library it removed 25 wrong labels and no correct ones: every take the model found before is still found, with the same start and end. The overall score moves only slightly, from 93.10% to 93.15% on a newer snapshot of 142 complete recordings, because those fragments are short.

v2.14 adds the chord-interval and note-gap features. Scored as single takes it looked like a step back, matching 39 fewer takes, because it joins more stretches of the same song. Scored as stretches of work, it gives far less playing to the wrong song (1,231 seconds against 2,910), splits half as many stretches and leaves about 40% fewer stray segments, for about 10% fewer review edits on both snapshots. It raises F1 from 93.15% to 94.22% on the September 23 snapshot and from 93.37% to 94.05% on the September 7 one.

Several plausible changes failed: equal prototype budgets discarded useful examples, one-hand training copies reduced accuracy, and stricter per-window rank checks broke takes into more fragments. None of the attempts to stop the model carrying a song across noodling worked without splitting real stretches as often: a larger “no song” budget, stricter decoding, shorter windows, and extra features for the middle of each window.
