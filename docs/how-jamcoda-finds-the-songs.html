How JamCoda finds the songs

A practice recording might contain a whole piece, three attempts at its ending, and five minutes of messing around. JamCoda uses the takes you’ve labelled to find familiar songs in the rest of your recordings and suggest where each one starts and stops.

It compares short stretches of MIDI with examples from your annotations, finds the phrases it recognises clearly, then joins them into takes. You review the results before they become annotations.

Six seconds at a time

JamCoda reads note pitches, start and release times, velocity, and sustain-pedal events. It doesn’t listen to audio or consult a score. The song names come from you.

The model examines six seconds of playing at a time, moving forward one second for each new window. That gives it some musical context without blurring every change of song. Six seconds worked best in experiments with windows from four to eight seconds.

Each window gets a label for its centre. A window covering 0–6 seconds describes what is playing at second 3; the next describes second 4. Those centres determine the initial song boundaries. A later step adjusts them to nearby notes.

What it remembers about the music

Each window becomes 37 numbers. Most describe which pitch classes are sounding: how much C, C♯, D, and so on. They are counted separately below middle C and from middle C upwards, so bass and treble patterns don’t get mixed together. This approximates the separation between accompaniment and melody; it doesn’t identify which hand played a note.

Longer notes contribute more, and louder notes receive more weight. The remaining numbers describe register, density, dynamics, note lengths, silence, and the regularity of note starts. The model retains these summaries, rather than the exact sequence of notes. Similar passages can therefore be hard to tell apart.

The sustain pedal matters. When you release a key with the pedal down, the model lets the note continue for up to 0.7 seconds, stopping sooner if the pedal lifts. Otherwise, lifting your hands to change position can look like silence even though the piano is still ringing.

Feature calculations

Each register’s pitch-class profile is normalised separately to sum to one. A note’s contribution is its duration inside the window, weighted by sqrt(velocity/127). The pedal tail contributes half that weight; its length is capped at 0.6 seconds above C5 and 0.7 seconds elsewhere.

Silence is the fraction of quarter-second bins with no sounding notes. Linking stops at a ratio of 0.7, but a four-second gap inside a six-second window reaches only about 0.67. Short pauses can slip through.

Learning from your annotations

Training keeps a sample of labelled windows, called prototypes. There is no neural network here: a prediction is based on resemblance to those stored examples. A separate __none__ label represents playing outside the named songs, including improvisation.

The model described here was trained from 1,007 annotations across 145 recordings. Those produced about 236,000 windows, reduced to 7,216 prototypes. A song with lots of annotated playing gets more examples, allowing the model to remember different passages and different ways of playing them.

That also creates a bias. With more examples, a song has more chances to produce a close match, even when it is the wrong answer. Attempts to compensate for this have reduced accuracy. Giving sparsely annotated songs more examples remains the practical way to improve their coverage.

Prototype selection and scoring

The default prototype budget is 8,000, divided between labels in proportion to the square root of their training-window counts. The __none__ label has a separate cap of 60. Within each label, training takes an evenly spaced, deterministic sample. Caps and available examples mean the saved total can be below the budget.

Features are standardised using the training mean and standard deviation. Each label’s score is the negative Euclidean distance to its nearest prototype: a closer match gives a higher score.

The decoder compares the best score with the runner-up using (top − runner-up) / (|top| + |runner-up|). This margin measures how decisively one label won. It is not a probability that the song is correct.

Joining the recognisable parts

A distinctive phrase is easy to recognise. A few bars of repeated chords may fit several songs. Labelling each window independently would break a performance into fragments whenever the music became less distinctive.

JamCoda first looks for anchors: at least three consecutive windows where the same song wins by a margin of 0.15 or more. It then extends those runs into the less certain passages around them. The current “bridge” decoder uses these rules:

  1. Join anchors of the same song. If two neighbouring anchor runs identify the same piece, extend into the gap from both ends. Silence and strong evidence for another label can still stop the extension.
  2. Limit extensions towards another song. Give an anchor two seconds of extension, then require its song to remain the top-scoring choice. This limit applies when a different-song anchor exists in that direction; open ends of the recording keep the ordinary linking checks.
  3. Let both sides advance. Competing extensions move one window at a time, so the earlier song doesn’t get first claim on the entire gap.
  4. Reconsider the gaps left over. A neighbouring song can claim a gap if it ranks well enough on average across that stretch. This rescue pass skips gaps with the same song on both sides, to avoid merging more repeated takes.

The last rule helps with passages the model partly recognises. A song may rarely win a window but still rank near the top throughout its own performance. The rescue threshold is a mean of at most five labels scoring above it; the code counts rank from zero.

Bridge linking became the training default in v2.11. Older saved models without a link-policy setting still use the previous decoder, so updating the app doesn’t silently change their predictions.

Turning the result into suggestions

Consecutive windows with the same song label become a segment. By default, segments shorter than eight seconds or below 0.3 confidence are dropped, and remaining same-song segments separated by at most five seconds are merged. Boundaries then snap to nearby note starts and releases. An ending-flourish heuristic can trim an arpeggio after the final chord.

Before suggestions enter the review queue, the app removes time you have already annotated. It also splits segments at device bookmarks and at recorded pauses of at least 30 seconds. These markers are applied after decoding; they don’t help the model identify the song.

You can confirm a suggestion, correct its name or boundaries, or reject it. Promoting an accepted suggestion makes it an annotation that the next rebuild can learn from. Rebuilding is a manual action. Marking a file complete clears its predictions and blocks further committed runs, though Prediction Lab can still preview results on it.

The displayed confidence has a known inconsistency: anchor windows keep their margins, while linked windows usually receive 0.5. A segment made mostly of linked windows can therefore score higher than one made of strong anchors. Treat it as a filtering value, not a calibrated likelihood.

Where it still needs help

Repeated takes merge. The same-song linking rule helps preserve a performance through an ambiguous passage. It can also join two separate attempts. Short pauses don’t always pass the silence threshold, and gap merging can join nearby segments again. Repeated takes are the largest remaining source of missed boundaries.

A weak middle can cut a take short. When recognition fades, the extension limit may stop a song too soon. The rescue pass can miss the rest because it averages the remaining music together with the unrelated playing after it. One Ashokan Farewell take was reduced to 14 seconds out of 78. An experimental rescue with a shorter lookahead helps some such cases, but gave no clear overall gain and remains off by default.

Warm-up makes the boundary debatable. In one recording, the annotations for The Entertainer begin 73 seconds in, while the model starts near the beginning. The disputed passage is warm-up on that piece. Deciding whether it counts as a take needs a consistent annotation rule; a score alone cannot settle it.

What improved

Increasing the number of stored examples improved coverage. Standardising features made distances less sensitive to outliers. Accounting for pedal sustain reduced false silence, and snapping boundaries to notes removed some of the loose edges left by the one-second grid.

Bridge linking mostly improved endings. On the same snapshot, it raised F1 from 91.16% to 93.33% and reduced median ending error from 5.85 seconds late to 0.81 seconds late, with recall nearly unchanged.

Several plausible changes failed: equal prototype budgets discarded useful examples, one-hand training copies reduced accuracy, and stricter per-window rank checks broke takes into more fragments. The ML changelog records those experiments and the release history.
